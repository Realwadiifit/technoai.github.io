01 — Method

Three things happen on every shift.

An assignment is a contract with a finish line. That is the whole discipline — and it is why the output arrives instead of stalling.

1

You write the instruction

Plain language, with the outcome and the acceptance bar. "Know the market well enough to price it" counts. Vague gets clarified before work starts, not after.

2

The employee works it

Unattended, in parallel, against real tools and real sources. Claims are checked against each other; weak evidence gets dropped rather than decorated.

3

Work product is signed off

You receive the file, the diff, or the live link — plus what was verified and what was not. Nothing is called done on a promise.

The report changed the plan. That is the only review that matters.

Most "AI employee" products sell you a chat window and call the hours you spend steering it a workflow. That is the wrong unit. A shift has an acceptance bar written before it starts, and the employee is judged on whether the thing arrived — not on how pleasant the exchange felt.

So the ledger above records output: sources fetched, claims kept, files filed. Anything unverifiable stays labelled as unverified, including when that makes the report shorter. A company that ships software under its own name cannot afford an assistant that guesses with confidence.

04 — Standing terms

House rules.

Written down because a shop with three employees and one owner still needs policy.

Output is owned by you

Every file, diff and report is handed over as yours. Nothing is held hostage inside a dashboard you must log into to read.

Nothing ships on a guess

Claims need sources; code needs a real build and a real run. If it was not tested, it gets marked assumed rather than passed.

No secrets in the walls

Credentials stay in your machine's own store. Assignments are logged; the log never carries a token, a key, or a password.
